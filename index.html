    Module 1 · Foundations

    Agent, host, environment

    What causes infection, and why do only some exposed people get sick?

    Infection happens when three things line up: an agent able to cause it, a host who is susceptible, and an environment that brings them together. Change any one of the three and you change the disease. Most public health work happens on the host side (vaccines, nutrition) and the environment side (water, housing, vectors).

    Think of it like this

    A fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen. Remove any one and it goes out. The pathogen is the spark. A crowded, unvaccinated population is dry fuel.

    • Pathogen types: viruses (measles, HIV, Ebola), bacteria (TB, cholera), parasites (malaria, schistosomiasis), fungi (Candida auris), prions.
    • Infection is not the same as disease. Most polio and most TB infections never cause illness.
    • Infectivity is how easily the agent infects. Pathogenicity is whether infection causes disease. Virulence is how severe that disease is.
    • Antimicrobial resistance is an agent factor that we make worse with every unnecessary antibiotic.

    Module 2 · Transmission

    The chain of infection

    How does a pathogen get from one person to the next, and where can we cut it?

    Every infection travels the same six links: agent → reservoir → portal of exit → mode of transmission → portal of entry → susceptible host. Breaking any single link stops transmission. The route decides which tool works: nets do nothing for measles, and masks do nothing for cholera.

    Think of it like this

    A relay race. The baton is the pathogen. You don't need to stop every runner. Make one hand-off fail and the race ends.

    Module 3 · Natural history

    The clock inside an infection

    When does someone become infectious, and when do they start to look sick?

    Two clocks start at infection. The incubation period runs until symptoms appear. The latent period runs until the person can infect others. If someone becomes infectious before they feel sick, symptom-based control (screening, isolating the ill) will always be a step behind.

    Think of it like this

    A smoke alarm that only sounds after the fire has spread to the next room. For COVID-19 the alarm is late. For Ebola it sounds roughly when the fire starts, which is why isolating sick people works so well.

    • Serial interval: time from symptom onset in one case to onset in the person they infected. You need it to estimate R.
    • Maximum incubation period sets how long contacts are followed up. Twice the maximum, with no new cases, is the usual rule for declaring an outbreak over (42 days for Ebola and Marburg).
    • Other outcomes: chronic carriage (hepatitis B, typhoid), latency and reactivation (TB, shingles), death.
    Module 4 · Measuring spread

    R₀, herd immunity and the epidemic curve

    How fast is it spreading, how big could it get, and how much vaccination stops it?

    R₀ is the average number of people one case infects in a fully susceptible population. Once immunity or control measures kick in, we talk about Rₜ. Above 1 the outbreak grows; below 1 it shrinks. If a share 1 − 1/R₀ of people are immune, each case infects fewer than one other person on average. That share is the herd immunity threshold.

    Think of it like this

    A chain letter. If each person forwards it to fewer than one new person on average, it dies out. Measles forwards to 12–18 people, so 19 in 20 must be immune before it stops.

    Module 5 · Surveillance and diagnostics

    Knowing what is happening

    How do we detect cases, and how far can we trust a positive test?

    Surveillance is information for action: collect, analyse, interpret, act. Tests feed it. A test's sensitivity and specificity belong to the test. Its positive predictive value (how often a positive is real) depends on how common the disease is where you use it.

    Think of it like this

    A smoke detector that sometimes beeps at toast. In a burning building almost every beep is a fire. In a quiet house almost every beep is toast. The detector is the same in both places.

    • Surveillance types: passive (routine reports), active (staff search for cases), sentinel (selected sites), syndromic, event-based (rumours, media), laboratory, genomic, wastewater.
    • Frameworks: IDSR in African countries, the International Health Regulations (2005), and DHIS2 as the common reporting platform.
    • Case definitions run suspected → probable → confirmed. Broad definitions catch more cases; narrow ones are more accurate.
    • Single-case alerts: cholera, measles, acute flaccid paralysis (polio), viral haemorrhagic fever and anthrax each trigger action on one case.
    • Serology: IgM suggests recent infection; IgG suggests past infection or immunity.
    Module 6 · Outbreak investigation

    From first alert to final report

    Cases are appearing. What do you do, and in what order?

    Investigations follow a well-worn sequence, but the steps overlap. Control measures start as soon as you know enough to act, not after the report. The epi curve is your first and most useful picture: its shape tells you how people were exposed.

    Detective work. The line list is the witness statements, the epi curve is the timeline of the crime, and the case-control study is the line-up.

    Module 7 · Prevention and control

    Stacking interventions

    Which levers break transmission, and which ones save lives?

    No single measure usually brings Rₜ below 1. Interventions multiply: each one removes a share of the transmission left over by the others. Some measures cut spread (water, vaccines); others cut deaths (treatment). A good response needs both.

    Think of it like this

    Slices of Swiss cheese. Each slice has holes, but stack enough of them and very little gets through.

    The effect sizes are illustrative, chosen to teach how measures combine. Real effects depend on coverage, quality and context. The WHO target for cholera treatment is a case fatality ratio below 1%.

    • Isolation separates sick people. Quarantine separates exposed people who are still well.
    • Vaccination: routine immunisation, mass campaigns, and ring vaccination around cases (as used for Ebola).
    • Infection prevention and control (IPC): standard precautions for every patient, plus extra precautions matched to the transmission route.
    • Mass drug administration and chemoprevention: seasonal malaria chemoprevention, deworming, PrEP.
    • Community engagement: interventions work only when people trust and use them.
    Module 8 · Priority diseases and One Health

    What to watch, and where new threats come from

    Which diseases matter most, and how do we catch the next one early?

    About 60% of known human pathogens, and about 75% of emerging ones, come from animals. One Health means human, animal and environmental health services sharing data and responding together. 7-1-7 gives every outbreak a clock: detect within 7 days, notify within 1, complete the early response within 7.

    Think of it like this

    Three neighbours each watching one side of the same house. A fire starting on any side is caught only if they talk to each other.

    • Global health security: IHR (2005), Joint External Evaluations, National Action Plans for Health Security, the Pandemic Fund, and the WHO Pandemic Agreement (adopted 2025).
    • Spillover drivers: land-use change, wildlife contact, intensive livestock farming, climate change, travel and trade.
    • Close to home: Rwanda's 2024 Marburg response went through the whole cycle: rapid detection, contact tracing, IPC in health facilities, a trial vaccine for high-risk contacts, then the 42-day countdown to declare the outbreak over.
